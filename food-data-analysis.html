Food Data Analysis (Capstone)

Nearly 80% of branded foods score negative.

I built a product-level nutrition score for USDA branded foods so shoppers can compare what actually matters at the grocery store: protein, fiber, sodium, saturated fat, and sugar per 100 calories.

Category Rankings

Some categories are built to score well. Others are fighting the math.

Fish, seafood, canned tuna, and vegetable-lentil mixes win because they bring protein without much sugar. Sauces, soda, honey, and sugar lose because calories arrive with little nutritional upside.

Highest scoringHigh protein relative to calories, low sugar, and manageable sodium.
Lowest scoringHigh sugar or sodium with little protein or fiber to offset it.
Why Scores Change

The same score can hide very different nutrition stories.

Soda is negative almost entirely because of sugar. Cheese has protein, but sodium and saturated fat pull it down. Tuna wins because protein is high and the penalties are low.

Cheese Decision

Cheese is not one decision. It is a product-level decision.

Swiss and American cheese have similar calorie and fat profiles, but sodium changes by about sevenfold.

253 mgmedian sodium for Swiss cheese
1,781 mgmedian sodium for American cheese
28.6gprotein in Swiss per serving
23.3gprotein in American per serving
Cheese Profiles

Switching hard cheeses may not reduce fat much.

Cheddar, Swiss, Gouda, Blue, and Brie cluster around 28 to 32g of fat. If the goal is protein with less fat, mozzarella is more interesting than a swap between hard cheeses.

Yogurt Decision

Greek means more protein, not automatically less sugar.

Greek yogurt has 8g protein, but 37 of its 81 calories come from sugar in the median product. Plain and Skyr are more reliable low-sugar cues than the Greek label alone.

Protein leaderSkyr/Icelandic yogurt: 10.0g protein per serving.
Sugar trapGreek yogurt is close to flavored yogurt on calories from sugar.
Snack Decision

A snack can be high-calorie and still be the better nutrition tradeoff.

Seeds and nuts are calorie-dense, but they also have the strongest protein signal and lower sodium than chips, pretzels, and crackers.

Product-Level Takeaway

Category labels are useful, but product-level scores make the decision actionable.

01

Use the score

NDS quickly flags products where sugar, sodium, and saturated fat outweigh protein and fiber.

02

Compare inside categories

Swiss vs. American matters more than ?cheese vs. no cheese? for sodium.

03

Read past health labels

Greek yogurt can still be sugar-heavy when most products are flavored.

04

Do not fear calories alone

Seeds and nuts bring calories, but also protein and lower sodium.

05

Rank products, not marketing

The front of the package becomes less persuasive when every product is scored the same way.

Data Quality Check

After the story, the boring-but-important part: the sodium data needed cleaning.

Only 0.11% of nutrient values were flagged, but sodium accounted for 82% of them. Cleaning lowered the sodium mean by 72% while the median barely moved.

Limits + Next Build

This is a transparent grocery-decision tool, not a complete nutrition label.

Total sugar proxy

Added sugar is missing for 69.6% of products, so total sugar can over-penalize dairy and fruit.

Equal weights

Protein, fiber, sodium, saturated fat, and sugar are weighted equally. Better weights would use outcome data or expert input.

Missing micronutrients

Calcium, iron, vitamin D, and other nutrients are not included yet.

Next build

Turn the score into a searchable product lookup so shoppers can compare options by category in seconds.
